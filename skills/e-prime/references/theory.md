# theory — History, rationale, evidence, criticism

## Origin

Alfred Korzybski's "Science and Sanity" (1933) attacked what he called the
"is of identity" and the "is of predication" — yet his own prose kept using
them in roughly 37% of its sentences (Bourland counted). In 1949, an
anonymous letter writer from Tolland, Connecticut suggested to the Institute
of General Semantics that the field simply abandon the verb altogether.
D. David Bourland, Jr., then a Korzybski fellow, took the suggestion
seriously: he rewrote a congress paper without a single `to be` token and
suffered a week-long headache for his trouble. He published the method
quietly in 1965 — "A Linguistic Note: Writing in E-Prime," General
Semantics Bulletin 32/33 — and named it with a formula: E′ = E − e, English
minus the forms of the verb.

Time magazine profiled the idea in May 1969. Three anthologies followed:
"To Be or Not: An E-Prime Anthology" (1991), "More E-Prime: To Be or Not II"
(1994), and "E-Prime III!: A Third Anthology" (1997).

## The semantic case

Korzybski's dictum: "whatever you say something is, it is not" — the map
never equals the territory. Identity claims (`X is Y`) commit premature
abstraction: "John is a farmer" collapses at least five distinct states —
John farms three acres; John owns and runs a 2,000-acre operation; John
receives subsidies for growing nothing; John just bought a farm; John has
farmed one spread for sixty-one years — into one frozen label. Predication
claims (`X is red`) project the observer's nervous system into the object;
the redness lives in the eye, not in the rose.

George Santayana, a decade before Korzybski:

> "The little word is has its tragedies: it names and identifies different things with the greatest innocence; and yet no two are ever identical."

Bertrand Russell made the structural point: the subject-predicate form has "rendered most philosophers incapable of giving any account of the world of science and daily life."

The verb carries a stasis illusion — "completeness, finality, and time
independence," in Bourland's words — that a process world never honors.

## What the ban does to prose

Bourland's four consequences:

1. **Vanishing pseudo-questions.** "What is art?", "Who am I?" cannot form.
   Better questions replace them: "How does this piece work on me?",
   "What can I do next?"
2. **Vanishing internal instructions.** "I am a failure, therefore…"
   loses its footing. Albert Ellis built a therapy pattern on exactly this
   move: rate the deed, never freeze the doer.
3. **Empty abbreviations exposed.** "It is clear that…",
   "Boys will be boys", "It's just semantics" — the ban drags their
   hollowness into view.
4. **Return of the role players.** The passive voice dies; agents return
   to sentences. Two scientific reports Bourland admired went from
   "the data were not available" to the truth: contractor personnel
   never switched on the antenna.

Kellogg and Bourland named the overall effect: the copula licenses a "deity mode of speech" that lets "even the most ignorant … transform their opinions magically into god-like pronouncements on the nature of things."

## Evidence

- **Political documents.** Bourland measured identity-plus-predication
  density across five texts: the US Constitution (~20% of sentences), the
  Communist Manifesto (~26%), Welch's Blue Book (~49%), Machiavelli's
  Prince (~54%), Aristotle's Politics (~60%). He read the ranking as a
  flexibility index — the least identity-bound document, the Constitution,
  proved the most durable in practice.
- **Classroom.** Korzybski had students replace self-labels like
  "I am depressed" with statements such as "I feel depressed when…", and
  reported a full letter-grade improvement against a comparison group.
- **Therapy.** Albert Ellis wrote two REBT books in E-Prime, though he
  later relaxed the rule for the sake of readability.
- **Composition.** Daniel Zimmerman's classroom method: students paraphrase
  about half their sentences through E-Prime and find their own voices in
  the process.
- **Letters.** David F. Maas rendered the New American Standard Bible into
  E-Prime (2009–2011); G. Spencer-Brown wrote "Laws of Form" (1969)
  without the verb, save one statement; David Gerrold teaches the
  discipline in a chapter of "Worlds of Wonder" — a chapter that also
  obeys its own rule.

## Robert Anton Wilson

Wilson wrote "Quantum Psychology" (1990) almost entirely in E-Prime and
made the wave/particle duality the showcase: "The photon is a wave" and
"The photon is a particle" contradict each other in standard English, yet
both describe real experiments. Reformulated — the photon behaves as a
wave under these instruments, as a particle under those — the contradiction
dissolves, and physics reads as operational description rather than
metaphysics. Wilson also reported his mentor Harry Weinberg's classroom
exercise: defend "John F. Kennedy is President of the United States"
mid-class on November 22, 1963. The lesson delivered itself.

Wilson's companion disciplines: "sombunall" (some but not all) against
allness, and "maybe logic" against two-valued certainty.

## Criticisms

- **James French, "The Top Ten Arguments Against E-Prime" (1992)** — the
  core objections: the ban exceeds the theory (it forbids harmless
  locative, auxiliary, and existential uses); removing a whole class of
  sentences narrows expressive range; context already neutralizes most
  identity and predication harm; identity sneaks back without the copula
  ("the silly ban on copula continues"); and identity-in-language differs
  from identification-in-reaction — general semantics already handles that
  link through indexing, dating, and quotation, while mathematics, the
  best time-binding language we have, runs on equivalence.
- **Ralph Kenyon, "E-Prime: The Spirit and the Letter" (1992)** —
  compliance without conversion: "exists as" and "qualifies as" merely
  re-spell identity; meanwhile a writer can keep the verb in its auxiliary
  uses and still avoid identification. His worked ladder: "I found the
  movie more rewarding than the novel" still buries the observer inside a
  discovery metaphor; "I liked the movie better than I liked the novel"
  tells the truth.
- **Robin Lakoff, Cullen Murphy (The Atlantic, 1992), Theresa Parkinson** —
  readability suffers under the ban; judgments grow harder to notice and
  therefore harder to refute; the community stays tiny.
- **Emory Menefee's E-Choice** — selective avoidance: drop the pernicious
  uses, keep the benign ones. He called the total ban "quixotic."
- **Stuart Mayper's E-Plus (1993)** — constructive surgery instead of
  amputation: assign distinct spellings to the verb's distinct functions
  rather than forbid them all.
- **ESL caution (John C. Herbert, US State Department)** — requiring the
  ban on every assignment "would deter students from developing other
  fundamental skills of fluent writing"; one assignment per term, though,
  forces students to think critically about grammar and vocabulary.
- **The smuggler's gallery** — attributive adjectives ("the squalid
  capitalists"), "as", "became", "find", and get-passives can all carry
  the old absolutes while the token count stays pristine.

## Variants

| Name | Rule | Champion |
|------|------|----------|
| strict E-Prime | zero `to be` tokens, always | Bourland |
| E-Choice | drop identity + predication only | Menefee |
| E-Plus | re-spell the verb by function instead of banning it | Mayper |
| eprune / E-Priming | stay alert, prune overuse, no cult of it | practitioners |
| minimalist forms | `is like` similes to break dogma quickly | Dan Scorpio's tutorial |
| zero-copula languages | no exact `to be` equivalent exists | Russian, Hungarian, Mandarin |
